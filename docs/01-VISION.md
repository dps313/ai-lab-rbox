# AI Lab Tycoon — Product Vision

**Status:** Draft 0.1 — review baseline, not a complete implementation specification  
**Phase:** Product definition / prototype validation  
**Last consolidated:** 2026-11-22

## Premise

**Start with $100 and an incomplete garage computer. Build the next AI empire.**

AI Lab Tycoon is a multiplayer Roblox tycoon about collecting computer hardware, assembling computing equipment, developing and publishing AI models, and expanding from a solo garage operation into a technology company.

It draws on the immediate collection and resale hooks of Roblox tycoons, the product-development decisions of *Game Dev Tycoon*, and the long-term strategy of a management game. It is **not** a realistic machine-learning simulator or an educational course.

## North-star player experience

A player finds hardware in a shared electronics store, builds a working computer, develops an AI product using understandable choices, sees an exciting release result, earns in-game money, and **wants to build the next version**. Over time they discover multiple routes to a successful company: research, trading, infrastructure, and business management.

The game succeeds because players want to develop their next model, discover their next rare component, or grow their space — not simply because a money counter is ticking.

## Core pillars

1. **Physical discovery and collecting.** Browse a social, slightly chaotic Goodwill-meets-Best-Buy computer store. Limited used inventory creates bargains and excitement; always-available new items keep progress possible.
2. **Product creation and ownership.** Players make and name AI products. Developing, training, releasing, and improving models is more personal than buying generic income multipliers.
3. **Accessible choices, deeper strategy.** A young or casual Roblox player understands the surface loop. Curious players discover trade-offs in training investments, performance, specialization, and costs.
4. **Tangible progression.** A garage becomes a beautiful workstation, startup office, server room, and ultimately a data center. Hardware, model releases, and collectibles look and feel rewarding.
5. **Gameplay evolves with automation.** The player initially performs work personally. Hiring, outsourcing, and infrastructure eventually replace repetitive activities with delegation and business decisions.
6. **Multiple viable approaches.** Research, hardware resale, compute infrastructure, and business strategy can overlap; no rigid classes or one required path.

## Audience and design tone

- **Roblox-first.** Immediate feedback, clear goals, attractive rarity/effects, and mobile-friendly interactions take priority over realism.
- **Lightly educational.** Players incidentally encounter accurate intuition about CPUs/GPUs, datasets, training, compute, inference, and operating costs. Do not force lessons or dense explanations. Educational value may appeal to parents, but that is an unvalidated marketing hypothesis.
- **Playful, aspirational, exaggerated.** The scale can grow from a junk PC to ridiculous futuristic infrastructure. Satirical corporate decisions and press conferences are possible future features, not MVP requirements.
- **Fair baseline progression.** Scarce items add opportunity, never permanently block a new player. The game should remain viable without Robux purchases.

## Product rules

- Reward players quickly and visibly; keep the first objective obvious.
- Tie upgrade value to capability, changed gameplay, or visible ownership.
- Preserve cause and effect: strategy matters; expensive hardware is not an unconditional model-quality multiplier.
- Prefer a few polished systems over many disconnected or trivial features.
- Avoid mandatory technical minigames and repetitive clicking without progression purpose.
- Design future expansion without prematurely implementing it.
- An idea is not a requirement. See `04-DESIGN-QUESTIONS.md`, `05-ROADMAP.md`, and `06-DECISIONS.md`.

## Important unresolved product decisions

The exact first model-building interaction, ratings and income formulas, progression pacing, number of earning model families, and relative importance of hardware collecting versus AI development are **not approved**. They require design and testing.
